## Purpose

The purpose of this document is to describe the Folklorama 2026 Educational Program as one coherent system.

It provides the architectural map that connects:

- the program’s educational purpose,
- the eight-module sequence,
- the progression of ideas,
- the development of language and cultural understanding,
- the relationship between Poland and Canada,
- the hierarchy of future lesson and material layers,
- the governance responsibilities of each document level.

This document is intended to support program authors, module authors, lesson designers, material developers, reviewers, governance reviewers and future maintainers.

It establishes how the parts of the program relate to one another before detailed implementation begins.

This document does not replace the Educational Framework, define the complete content of individual modules, replace the Module Specification Standard, serve as a Module Specification, define lessons or activities, authorize material production or define visual layouts.

The Educational Framework explains what the program is intended to achieve. The Program Architecture explains how the complete program is organized to achieve it.

## Program Narrative

The Folklorama 2026 Educational Program presents Poland as a place that participants gradually learn to locate, recognize, hear, experience, explore and understand.

The program begins with a broad invitation:

> Poland is a living place. Let us discover it.

Participants first establish a shared point of orientation. They encounter Poland as a country in Europe, as a place where people live contemporary lives and as a place connected in different ways with people and communities in Canada.

They then move from orientation to recognition. They explore how countries, communities and individuals use official, cultural and personal symbols to express identity and belonging.

Once Poland becomes more recognizable, participants encounter Polish as a living language. Language is not presented as an isolated school subject, but as a way to greet, respond, connect and participate.

The journey then moves into shared cultural life. Participants explore how people celebrate, remember, gather and maintain traditions, while recognizing that traditions change across families, regions, generations and contemporary contexts.

The program expands from celebration into creative expression. Music, dance and arts show that culture can be heard, seen, performed, interpreted and continually recreated.

Participants then encounter culture through sensory and social experience. Food becomes a way to explore memory, place, family, hospitality, adaptation and cultural exchange without reducing Polish culture to a list of dishes.

After building these cultural points of reference, participants travel conceptually through Poland. They explore landscapes, cities, communities and places as parts of a varied country rather than as a catalogue of administrative regions.

The program concludes with contemporary Poland. Participants encounter a country that continues to develop, create and contribute through science, technology, sport, culture, innovation and everyday life.

```text
We meet Poland.
↓
We learn how Poland can be recognized.
↓
We hear and use its language.
↓
We discover how people celebrate and maintain traditions.
↓
We experience culture through music, movement and art.
↓
We explore culture through taste, memory and gathering.
↓
We travel through Poland’s places and landscapes.
↓
We encounter Poland as a contemporary and evolving country.
```

The final understanding is not that the participant has completed Poland. Poland is complex, living and connected to the wider world, and there is always more to discover.

## Essential Question Progression

### Module 01 — Orientation

What is Poland? Where is it? Who lives there? What makes a country more than a place on a map? What would we like to discover?

### Module 02 — Recognition

How do people recognize Poland? What makes something a symbol? How can symbols express belonging? Can the same symbol mean different things to different people?

### Module 03 — Communication

How does language help people connect? What can we communicate with a few meaningful words? How can we participate when we know different amounts of Polish? What makes language feel welcoming?

### Module 04 — Tradition

Why do people celebrate? How do traditions connect people with memory and community? Why can the same tradition look different across families? How do traditions continue and change?

### Module 05 — Creative Expression

How can music, movement and art communicate meaning? What can creative forms tell us about people and places? How do artists and communities renew tradition? How can one work be interpreted differently?

### Module 06 — Food and Social Meaning

How can food tell a story? Why do foods change across families, places and generations? How are meals connected with hospitality and community? What can taste and memory help us understand?

### Module 07 — Place and Diversity

How do landscapes and places shape everyday life? How can one country contain many environments and communities? What makes a place meaningful? How can travel change understanding?

### Module 08 — Contemporary Poland

What is Poland like today? How does a country carry history while changing? How do people in Poland create and contribute now? What might we discover next?

Program-level progression:

```text
What is it?
↓
How do we recognize it?
↓
How can we participate?
↓
Why does it matter to people?
↓
How is it expressed?
↓
How is it experienced?
↓
How does place shape it?
↓
How is it changing?
```

## Vocabulary Progression

Vocabulary must be defined within individual Module Specifications and must not become one cumulative memorization list.

Principles:

1. words return when they gain additional meaning,
2. new vocabulary grows from established concepts,
3. Polish and English are connected through use and context,
4. vocabulary supports cultural understanding rather than isolated language work.

### Module 01 — Orientation Vocabulary

Country, map, Europe, Poland, Canada, people, place, language, welcome and discovery.

### Module 02 — Recognition and Identity Vocabulary

Symbol, flag, emblem, colours, identity, belonging, official, personal, community and meaning.

### Module 03 — Communication Vocabulary

Greetings, names, polite expressions, simple questions and responses, feelings, needs, participation language and everyday actions.

### Module 04 — Celebration Vocabulary

Celebration, tradition, family, community, memory, season, gathering, custom, preparation and change.

### Module 05 — Creative Vocabulary

Music, rhythm, song, dance, movement, pattern, colour, artist, create and interpret.

### Module 06 — Sensory and Social Vocabulary

Taste, smell, ingredients, meal, recipe, table, hospitality, memory, family variation and regional variation.

### Module 07 — Place Vocabulary

City, town, village, region, sea, mountains, lakes, rivers, landscape, journey, direction and environment.

### Module 08 — Contemporary Vocabulary

Contemporary, innovation, science, technology, sport, culture, media, design, future, change and contribution.

Recurring words may include Polska / Poland, Kanada / Canada, ludzie / people, kultura / culture, język / language, społeczność / community, odkrywać / to discover, tworzyć / to create, tradycja / tradition and współczesny / contemporary.

A recurring word should return with a new context, more precise meaning, new communicative use or connection with another concept.

## Language Progression

The program is not a linear Polish-language course. Language progression describes increasing opportunity for meaningful use, not a requirement that all participants reach the same level.

### Stage 1 — Encounter: Module 01

Participants hear or see selected Polish words, recognize Polish as a living language, connect words with meaningful ideas and may try simple greetings.

### Stage 2 — Recognition and Naming: Module 02

Participants recognize names of selected symbols and concepts, associate terms with visual and cultural meaning and begin naming familiar elements.

### Stage 3 — Functional Participation: Module 03

Participants use selected words and expressions to greet, respond, ask or participate.

### Stage 4 — Contextual Language: Modules 04–06

Participants use language connected with celebration, creative expression and sensory experience, describe observations, express preferences where appropriate and compare experiences.

### Stage 5 — Description and Interpretation: Module 07

Participants describe places and landscapes, use relational and geographical vocabulary and communicate observations with increasing precision.

### Stage 6 — Synthesis and Expression: Module 08

Participants discuss contemporary themes at an appropriate level, connect ideas, express questions or opinions and identify further areas of exploration.

#### No Polish

Access and recognition → optional word attempts → supported contextual understanding → confidence in encountering Polish.

#### Beginner

Recognition → single words → short supported phrases → simple contextual communication.

#### Intermediate

Short descriptions → questions and comparisons → connected statements → simple interpretation.

#### Advanced

Precise description → explanation → interpretation → discussion of complexity and perspective.

Language level must not be treated as equivalent to cultural knowledge, age, intelligence, identity or commitment.

## Cultural Progression

1. **Culture as Living Context — Module 01:** Culture belongs to people and everyday life.
2. **Culture as Recognition and Identity — Module 02:** Official, community, cultural and personal symbols communicate meaning.
3. **Culture as Communication — Module 03:** Language carries social context and ways of participating.
4. **Culture as Continuity and Variation — Module 04:** Traditions connect people with memory but change across families and generations.
5. **Culture as Creation — Module 05:** Culture is performed, interpreted, adapted and renewed.
6. **Culture as Sensory and Relational Experience — Module 06:** Taste, memory, hospitality and gathering become cultural lenses.
7. **Culture as Place-Based Experience — Module 07:** Geography, settlement and local experience create internal diversity.
8. **Culture as Contemporary Life — Module 08:** Polish culture includes tradition but is not limited to it and continues to develop.

```text
first contact
→
recognition
→
participation
→
continuity
→
expression
→
personal and social experience
→
place-based diversity
→
contemporary synthesis
```

## Canada Connections Progression

### Module 01 — Canada as the Starting Context

Canada helps participants locate Poland geographically and personally through family, community, migration, friendship, events or curiosity.

### Module 02 — Canada as a Context for Multiple Identities

Participants explore layered belonging, different symbols in different settings and varied interpretation.

### Module 03 — Canada as a Multilingual Environment

Participants understand that people use different languages, families may be multilingual and participation does not require fluency.

### Module 04 — Canada as a Place Where Traditions Travel and Change

Traditions move with people, adapt, change across generations and appear differently in multicultural settings.

### Module 05 — Canada as a Place of Cultural Expression and Exchange

Music, dance and art help communities maintain connections, present culture publicly, collaborate and participate in Folklorama.

### Module 06 — Canada as a Place of Adaptation and Hospitality

Recipes, ingredients and family practices adapt while cultural traditions are shared.

### Module 07 — Canada as a Comparative Geographic Context

Participants may compare distance, climate, landscapes, cities, regions, travel and relationships with place without ranking.

### Module 08 — Canada as a Context for Continuing Relationships

Poland–Canada connections may involve education, travel, culture, research, technology, sport, media, community and personal interest.

## Material Layer Architecture

```text
Program
↓
Module
↓
Lesson
↓
Activity
↓
Educational Material Types
├── Worksheet
├── Comic
├── Colouring Book
├── Teacher Guide
└── Family Materials
```

The listed material types are anticipated implementation categories. Their inclusion does not authorize production, and not every lesson or activity requires every type.

### Program Layer

Defines purpose, audiences, narrative, module sequence, competencies, bilingual approach, cultural direction and system-wide success criteria.

### Module Layer

Defines module role, scope, Big Idea, Essential Questions, Learning Outcomes, Core Topics, vocabulary, differentiation and module-level cultural and Canadian connections.

### Lesson Layer

Will define a bounded learning unit within a module, including purpose, relation to outcomes, content focus, age and language entry points, delivery conditions, experience categories, evidence of engagement and material requirements.

### Activity Layer

Will define a specific participant experience, including educational purpose, participant action, relationship to outcomes, accessibility, age and language adaptation, resources and safety or facilitation considerations where relevant.

### Worksheet Layer

Supports recording, matching, observing, organizing, reflecting, practising selected language or communicating an idea. It must not become the curriculum itself.

### Comic Layer

May support cultural context, language encounter, approved character interaction, observation, discussion and emotional or social engagement. It must not introduce unapproved scope, claims or characters.

### Colouring Book Layer

May support visual recognition, observation, engagement, vocabulary reinforcement and creative interpretation. It must not reduce culture to decoration.

### Teacher Guide Layer

Supports consistent implementation through educational intent, preparation, facilitation notes, cultural context, language support, differentiation, accuracy notes and inclusion considerations. It implements approved specifications and does not replace them.

### Family Materials Layer

Extends the program into optional family contexts through explanation, conversation, shared discovery and accessible bilingual context. It must not require Polish, ancestry disclosure or obligatory participation.

### Layer Integrity Rule

Each lower layer implements decisions above it. It may add detail but may not change program purpose, module ownership, outcomes, terminology, cultural or language-access requirements, brand elements or verification requirements.

Problems discovered at a lower layer must be escalated rather than silently reinterpreted.

## Risks

### Content Duplication

The same topics may be fully developed in multiple modules. Control through ownership, introduces-but-does-not-develop rules, progression and adjacent-module review.

### Loss of Program Narrative

Modules may become unrelated thematic stations. Control through the Program Narrative, Narrative Threads, transitions and purposeful recurring concepts.

### Encyclopedic Overload

Authors may try to include everything important about Poland. Control through Big Ideas, limited Essential Content, explicit exclusions and Future Extensions.

### Separation of Language From Culture

Polish may become isolated vocabulary while cultural modules operate only in English. Control through meaningful language use, Language Outcomes and access for participants with no Polish.

### Cultural Reduction

Poland may be reduced to food, costume, colours, historical buildings or famous figures. Control through balance of tradition and contemporary life, ordinary experience, variation and whole-program representation review.

### Inconsistent Module Depth

Some modules may become mini-curricula while others remain outlines. Control through one standard, comparative review and role-based balance.

### Vocabulary Inflation

Long unprioritized lists may create overload and anxiety. Control through Core, Supporting and Extension levels, meaningful use and cumulative review.

### Inconsistent Language Accessibility

Some modules may assume Polish knowledge. Control through four entry points, English access to essential meaning and participant-facing terminology review.

### Age Differentiation Becoming Separate Programs

Three age versions may become unrelated. Control through one module identity and changes only to complexity, language, independence and reasoning.

### Material-Driven Curriculum

Worksheets, comics or visual ideas may begin determining educational content. Control through approved upstream specifications and clear educational purpose.

### Inconsistent Poland–Canada Connections

Canada may be central in some modules and decorative in others. Control through progression and module-specific relevance.

### Historical or Contemporary Inaccuracy

Outdated data, unsupported claims or oversimplified history may reduce trust. Control through verification, source recording and consultation.

### Governance Drift

Documents may be renamed, revised or approved without hierarchy control. Control through metadata, statuses, parent records and version traceability.

### Optimizing Individual Modules at the Expense of the System

A strong standalone module may weaken overall progression. Control through system-level review and distinct role protection.

## Future Evolution

The architecture supports responsible development after Folklorama.

Possible extensions include deeper module pathways, school-based versions, home learning, community workshops, digital adaptations, seasonal versions, alternative delivery models, expanded age groups, expanded language pathways, regional and community extensions, digital tools, additional material types and future program editions.

Reuse beyond Folklorama may include school programming, community events, family resources, online learning, cultural orientation and teacher development.

Future development must:

1. preserve distinctions between program, module, lesson, activity and material,
2. extend approved structures rather than create parallel systems,
3. maintain language–culture integration,
4. protect accessibility across language levels,
5. connect contemporary life with tradition,
6. preserve Canada as a meaningful context,
7. verify new facts and representations,
8. record structural changes through Governance,
9. prefer long-term system value over novelty,
10. ensure every new element has a defined role and avoids unnecessary duplication.

Changes to module sequence, number of modules, module roles, program narrative, primary content ownership, language model or the Poland–Canada relationship require architecture-level review.
